# Structure (21 principles)

Structure is the scaffolding that turns scattered design facts into a sequence an audience can follow and feel. These principles supply ready-made shapes - arcs, spines, hierarchies, rhythms - that decide what comes first, where the turn belongs, and how the story lands. Use them to organize a pitch, a case study, a service journey, or a full product narrative.

### 8. Ceremonies and Rituals
**In one line:** Shared rituals - critique, stand-ups, unboxing - focus attention, bind people, and seed a design story with repeatable meaning.
**Use it when:** Pitching studio culture; a product with a celebrated unboxing; a service whose recurring customer moments deserve staging.
**Apply it:**
- List the client's recurring rituals, then mark each as a named beat in the deck's storyline.
- Storyboard one ritual step by step, from sealed package to first use, as an intentional sequence.
- Add a scene showing the team's own ceremony and the standard it enforces.
**Watch out:** Describing a ritual without showing its effect on the work reads as corporate theater.
**Pairs with:** 10 Choreography, 31 Functions of Storytelling, 48 Master Planning

### 10. Choreography
**In one line:** Sequence and stage every interaction - click, pause, path - so the design's flow reads like choreographed movement.
**Use it when:** Mapping a user journey; explaining a service flow to a client; showing how a store or airport guides people.
**Apply it:**
- Write the key interactions as a timed sequence - click, drag, drop, pause - on one slide.
- Draw the flow as a transit-style diagram, enlarging critical stations and compressing minor steps.
- Mark allowed and forbidden moves at each step, like a choreographer blocking a scene.
**Watch out:** Listing screens without timing and sequence leaves the experience flat and hard to follow.
**Pairs with:** 8 Ceremonies and Rituals, 68 Rhythm and Silence, 55 Narrative Design

### 12. Contrast, Beginning to End
**In one line:** Show a visible gap between the ordinary world before your design and the changed reality after it.
**Use it when:** A redesign pitch needs a before/after; a case study opens on results; stakeholders can't see why change matters.
**Apply it:**
- Open the deck with a two-panel before/after of the user's world, solution still hidden.
- Write one sentence for the status quo and one for life after launch; place them side by side.
- Cut every middle detail until only start state and end state remain.
**Watch out:** Starting with the solution instead of the problem makes the transformation impossible to appreciate.
**Pairs with:** 73 Show, Don't Tell, 88 The Trigger or Wicked Problem, 95 Transformation

### 20. Disney's Twelve Basic Principles of Animation
**In one line:** Twelve animation fundamentals - anticipation, staging, timing, appeal - that make interfaces, decks, and motion feel alive.
**Use it when:** Animating UI transitions; building a motion spec; polishing a deck or product demo that feels stiff.
**Apply it:**
- Label each animation with its principle - anticipation, slow-in/slow-out, follow-through - in the motion spec.
- Add easing and a small pre-motion cue to entrances so they read as physical.
- Check that every key frame carries one focal action plus supporting secondary motion.
**Watch out:** Motion without anticipation or clear staging draws attention to itself, not the message.
**Pairs with:** 10 Choreography, 52 Mise en Scène, 68 Rhythm and Silence

### 25. Essence Model
**In one line:** Define a design's essence through four lenses: observable attributes, intent, how it makes users appear, how it feels.
**Use it when:** Boiling a brand to its core; checking a positioning statement; deciding which product facts belong in a pitch.
**Apply it:**
- Write four statements for the artifact: what it is, what it does, how users look, how it feels.
- Delete attributes one at a time until recognition breaks; keep what survives in the copy.
- Surface one feeling-level benefit per section instead of stacking feature lists.
**Watch out:** Listing only factual traits produces a spec sheet, not a story people feel.
**Pairs with:** 5 Archetypes, 29 Form and Function, 47 Maslow's Hierarchy of Need

### 29. Form and Function
**In one line:** Balance what a design looks like with what it does; the narrative has to prove both.
**Use it when:** The audience loves visuals but asks if it works; engineers dismiss pretty work; copy hypes looks without proof.
**Apply it:**
- Pair each aesthetic claim with the purpose it serves, sentence by sentence, in the pitch copy.
- Lead with a one-line purpose statement before any visual showcase.
- Rebalance the deck for the room: mechanics first for engineers, aesthetics first for luxury buyers.
**Watch out:** Praising the look while ignoring reliability leaves audiences unsure the design actually performs.
**Pairs with:** 19 Dieter Rams' Principles of Design, 25 Essence Model, 41 Jobs to Be Done

### 30. Freytag's Pyramid
**In one line:** A five-stage arc - exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement - built to release catharsis.
**Use it when:** A case study feels flat; a campaign film needs an emotional peak; the ending lands without release.
**Apply it:**
- Outline the case study in the five named stages and flag any stage that is missing.
- Place the redesign's turning point as the climax, with stakes before and consequences after.
- End the deck with a denouement slide showing the new normal and the emotional release.
**Watch out:** Skipping a stage so the story evokes no catharsis leaves it feeling incomplete.
**Pairs with:** 62 Pixar's Story Structure, 72 Seven-Point Story Structure, 93 Three-Act Structure

### 31. Functions of Storytelling
**In one line:** Stories work several jobs at once - entertain, teach, warn, explain, preserve culture, persuade - so choose yours.
**Use it when:** Justifying storytelling to a skeptical client; deciding what a campaign film is for; aligning a team on tone.
**Apply it:**
- State the story's job - teach, warn, persuade, celebrate - in one line atop the brief.
- Audit each content piece against the function list; cut or rewrite anything serving no clear function.
- Translate the client's internal, unspoken narrative into outward messaging the audience can hear.
**Watch out:** A story with no chosen function entertains nobody and changes nothing.
**Pairs with:** 7 Calls to Action, 8 Ceremonies and Rituals, 57 Narrative Transportation

### 42. Kano and Storytelling
**In one line:** Sort features into basics, performance, and WOW tiers to decide which part of the product earns the story.
**Use it when:** The feature list is bloated; a launch story needs a hook; competitors match the same specs.
**Apply it:**
- Tag every feature in the deck as basic, performance, WOW, indifferent, or reverse before writing copy.
- Move indifferent and reverse claims out of the pitch; they drain energy and invite objections.
- Stage the WOW moment as a concrete scene - cookie on the pillow - not a bullet.
**Watch out:** Promising basics as if they were delights earns no credit; their absence only earns blame.
**Pairs with:** 25 Essence Model, 29 Form and Function, 41 Jobs to Be Done

### 44. Kishōtenketsu
**In one line:** A four-act structure - introduction, development, twist, reconciliation - that builds meaning without conflict.
**Use it when:** Presenting a design team or portfolio; audiences dislike adversarial framing; a story needs a turn, not a villain.
**Apply it:**
- Lay the pitch out in four labeled sections: who we are, what we do, the twist, the resolution.
- Write the twist as an unexpected research finding or constraint that forced a better method.
- Close by showing how the twist reconnects to the opening, without claiming anyone was defeated.
**Watch out:** Forcing a villain into kishōtenketsu turns it back into ordinary conflict-driven storytelling.
**Pairs with:** 12 Contrast, Beginning to End, 72 Seven-Point Story Structure, 92 Thesis + Antithesis = Synthesis

### 47. Maslow's Hierarchy of Need
**In one line:** Map the design onto five levels of human need, from essential function up to self-actualization.
**Use it when:** Positioning benefit tiers; a client asks why anyone needs this; the pitch sounds purely functional.
**Apply it:**
- Draw a blank pyramid and mark which level each feature or message addresses.
- Order the deck's benefit stack from essential functionality up to empowerment and growth.
- Claim multiple levels explicitly when the design spans them, naming the role at each.
**Watch out:** Pitching only self-actualization benefits while basic reliability is unproven loses the audience.
**Pairs with:** 6 Aspiration, 25 Essence Model, 41 Jobs to Be Done

### 48. Master Planning
**In one line:** Read a brand or service as a city with six places: marketplace, park, temple, school, stage, neighborhoods.
**Use it when:** Auditing an ecosystem of pages and services; a brand feels fragmented; planning a site map or service journey.
**Apply it:**
- Score the ecosystem against the six places and mark each present, weak, or missing.
- Give every section a job - buy, rest, admire, learn, gather, navigate - and label it on the sitemap.
- Add breathing room where information piles up, treating white space as the city's park.
**Watch out:** A full inventory with nowhere to rest overwhelms the people moving through it.
**Pairs with:** 10 Choreography, 55 Narrative Design, 100 World-Building

### 55. Narrative Design
**In one line:** Write the whole experience as plain text, step by step, before building any high-fidelity prototype.
**Use it when:** Concept stage with no prototyping budget; aligning design with development; testing acceptability with real users.
**Apply it:**
- Draft the flow as numbered user actions and system responses, one screen per paragraph.
- Bold the speech acts - select, tap, confirm, pay - and build a shared vocabulary list.
- Send the text flow to users or stakeholders for acceptability feedback before any visual work.
**Watch out:** Skipping speech acts leaves developers guessing at what each screen must actually do.
**Pairs with:** 45 Lexicon, 48 Master Planning, 65 Prototyping

### 62. Pixar's Story Structure
**In one line:** Fill a five-line story spine - ordinary world, disruption, consequences, resolution - to draft any narrative fast.
**Use it when:** Writing an elevator pitch; drafting a problem statement; a presentation needs structure in minutes.
**Apply it:**
- Fill the five blanks in order: who and where, the daily routine, the disrupting event, its consequences, the resolution.
- Chain several "because of that" lines to show cause and effect between design decisions.
- Convert the finished spine directly into the deck's problem statement and narrative arc.
**Watch out:** Skipping "every day" removes the status quo, so nothing later feels like real change.
**Pairs with:** 12 Contrast, Beginning to End, 63 Plot, 93 Three-Act Structure

### 69. Rule of Three
**In one line:** Group ideas in threes - the smallest number that reads as a pattern - and put the strongest last.
**Use it when:** Writing headlines or taglines; structuring a slide around three points; a feature list runs long.
**Apply it:**
- Cut any list to three items, placing the most persuasive claim in position three.
- Rewrite the value proposition as a tricolon of three matched phrases.
- Compress three supporting words into one idea with hendiatris for the campaign tagline.
**Watch out:** A fourth item breaks the pattern; audiences stop remembering and start writing it down.
**Pairs with:** 7 Calls to Action, 63 Plot, 68 Rhythm and Silence

### 71. Seven Basic Plots
**In one line:** Match the design challenge to one of seven classic plots to give it a familiar, resonant shape.
**Use it when:** A case study lacks narrative; choosing a campaign concept; deciding how to frame a research story.
**Apply it:**
- Label the project with one plot - Overcoming the Monster, Quest, Rebirth - and outline it in that frame.
- Rewrite the case study opening to establish that plot's setup in the first two paragraphs.
- Use Tragedy for post-mortem narratives, framing failure as a lesson never repeated.
**Watch out:** Forcing a plot that doesn't fit the facts makes the story feel borrowed and untrue.
**Pairs with:** 5 Archetypes, 63 Plot, 83 The Hero's Journey

### 72. Seven-Point Story Structure
**In one line:** Run the seven-point structure backward - start at the resolution and work toward exposition to generate ideas.
**Use it when:** Brainstorming after research; the outcome is known but the story isn't; checking a pitch for holes.
**Apply it:**
- Write the ideal transformed outcome first, then step back through pinch points and plot points to the inciting incident.
- List the last-minute obstacles that could derail success and keep only the most threatening ones.
- Finish with exposition and verify the who, why, what, and where support the outcome you began with.
**Watch out:** Working backward without checking the inciting incident leaves a resolution the story never earns.
**Pairs with:** 30 Freytag's Pyramid, 81 The Fichtean Curve, 93 Three-Act Structure

### 81. The Fichtean Curve
**In one line:** Skip the setup and open on a stack of escalating crises, saving exposition for later.
**Use it when:** Presenting wicked problems after discovery research; urgency matters; several failures share one root.
**Apply it:**
- Open the report with at least three linked problems, each a symptom of the next, no background yet.
- Give rising action about two-thirds of the deck, then land one climax tied to the first problem.
- Close on unresolved tension and convert it into an explicit call to action.
**Watch out:** Fichtean leaves some problems unsolved; without a call to action the audience just feels bleak.
**Pairs with:** 11 Conflict, 7 Calls to Action, 88 The Trigger or Wicked Problem

### 83. The Hero's Journey
**In one line:** Follow the hero from ordinary world through call, mentor, ordeal, and return with a hard-won solution.
**Use it when:** A founder or client is the protagonist; a project narrative needs transformation; pitching a journey, not features.
**Apply it:**
- Cast the client as hero and the studio as mentor; cast the constraint as the cave.
- Name each stage in the deck - call, threshold, ordeal, reward - with one supporting scene.
- End with the return: the elixir the client brings back as the new status quo.
**Watch out:** Making the agency the hero steals the client's story and weakens their buy-in.
**Pairs with:** 30 Freytag's Pyramid, 71 Seven Basic Plots, 72 Seven-Point Story Structure

### 93. Three-Act Structure
**In one line:** Split the story into setup, struggle, and resolution, each anchored by at least one clear story beat.
**Use it when:** Pitching a redesign; a deck needs a simple spine; a case study reads as a list of tasks.
**Apply it:**
- Label three sections: status quo and inciting incident, work and setbacks, final conflict and new normal.
- Underline one story beat per act and check that each causes the next.
- State the catalyst numerically - conversions dropping, six months of runway - so act one has stakes.
**Watch out:** Acts without cause and effect become three slides, not a story.
**Pairs with:** 30 Freytag's Pyramid, 62 Pixar's Story Structure, 83 The Hero's Journey

### 97. Vonnegut's Shapes of Stories
**In one line:** Plot a story's fortunes on good-bad versus time to reveal its shape, then pick a shape for the design.
**Use it when:** Choosing an emotional arc; checking whether a campaign film rises or sinks; mapping persona journeys.
**Apply it:**
- Draw the arc on two axes - good versus bad vertically, beginning to end horizontally.
- Name the project's shape - Man in a Hole, From Bad to Worse, Cinderella - and label the slide with it.
- Plot each persona's life with the design as the turning point.
**Watch out:** A flat line means nothing changes; the design story needs visible movement in fortune.
**Pairs with:** 30 Freytag's Pyramid, 83 The Hero's Journey, 93 Three-Act Structure
