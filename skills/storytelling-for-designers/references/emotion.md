# Emotion (15 principles)

Emotion is what turns a design story from something an audience merely understands into something they act on and retell. These principles cover feeling as decision evidence, how audiences attach to characters, and the pacing, sensory, and exposure techniques that make a narrative land and stay remembered.

### 1. Affect as Information Theory
**In one line:** Treat emotion as usable evidence in a decision, not decoration on top of rational facts.
**Use it when:** Audiences can't weigh every feature; a buyer picks between near-identical vendors; a stakeholder judges a deck by gut reaction.
**Apply it:**
- Swap one jargon-heavy spec paragraph for plain sensory copy about how the product feels in use.
- Open the pitch deck with two slides on how the customer feels after the service.
- Tag each emotional claim in the report with a testable proof point so feeling and fact stay separable.
**Watch out:** Assuming your own reaction is universal; test with several audiences.
**Pairs with:** 22 Emotion, 23 Empathy, 57 Narrative Transportation

### 6. Aspiration
**In one line:** Frame the design as the vehicle that carries an audience toward a goal they already want.
**Use it when:** A brand needs a purpose-led campaign; customers want to be greener or fitter; a roadmap sells a future state, not features.
**Apply it:**
- Rewrite the About page so purpose precedes specs, naming who the work helps and what they can then achieve.
- Add a deck slide stating the client's aspiration in their own words, then show the design removing one obstacle.
- Rewrite three feature-first lines of campaign copy as aspirational outcomes.
**Watch out:** Reciting the aspiration without proving the design actually advances it.
**Pairs with:** 23 Empathy, 47 Maslow's Hierarchy of Need, 95 Transformation

### 9. Character Relatability
**In one line:** Give every character motives, flaws, and an origin so audiences connect with the design story.
**Use it when:** Introducing a persona or founder in a deck; a new brand has no history; a case study's subject is anonymous.
**Apply it:**
- Add an origin block to the case study: why the design exists, what shaped it, what it wants.
- Give the product one flaw and one unexpected detail in the deck so it reads as a character.
- Reveal benefits in stages across slides to build intrigue instead of dumping them at once.
**Watch out:** Making the character relatable but boring; withhold detail to create urgency.
**Pairs with:** 5 Archetypes, 28 Foreshadowing, 98 Vulnerability

### 16. Deceptive Cadence
**In one line:** Withhold a key reveal, then break the expected pattern to recapture attention at the close.
**Use it when:** A long case study is losing the room; a deck's ending feels predictable; a campaign needs a twist people retell.
**Apply it:**
- Move the strongest payoff - cost savings, impact number - to the final slide instead of opening with it.
- Rewrite the mid-deck section so the tone shifts sharply: calm setup, then one reframing line.
- In the landing page hero, promise one expected benefit, then surprise with an unannounced one below the fold.
**Watch out:** Telegraphing the twist early, which flattens the surprise.
**Pairs with:** 68 Rhythm and Silence, 75 Stickiness, 86 The Peak-End Rule

### 22. Emotion
**In one line:** Aim every design story at a specific feeling, then give the audience somewhere to act on it.
**Use it when:** A campaign needs measurable engagement; a flat report isn't being shared; copy risks reading as manipulative rather than moving.
**Apply it:**
- Pair the emotional peak with a concrete call to action so the feeling converts to a step.
- Rewrite vague adjectives into vivid, sensory specifics, and introduce conflict where the story currently reads smoothly.
- Test the piece with several audiences to confirm the intended feeling actually lands.
**Watch out:** Tipping into manipulation; never engineer a feeling you can't justify.
**Pairs with:** 1 Affect as Information Theory, 7 Calls to Action, 95 Transformation

### 23. Empathy
**In one line:** Move from knowing audience data to understanding the people behind it, then design from that understanding.
**Use it when:** Research shows demographics but not motivations; a service feels cold; you're proposing changes to someone else's lived experience.
**Apply it:**
- Add a report section contrasting what the data says with what the user is actually trying to do.
- Rewrite onboarding copy from the user's context - what they fear, what they want - rather than the org's process.
- Distill interview notes into three plain statements of need, placed above the solution in the deck.
**Watch out:** Mistaking demographic data for understanding; knowledge alone doesn't reveal latent needs.
**Pairs with:** 22 Emotion, 58 Observing, 61 Phenomenology

### 39. Infrathin
**In one line:** Name the barely-perceptible difference that makes one design feel unlike an otherwise identical rival.
**Use it when:** Six competitors offer the same product; a brand refresh needs a defensible nuance; a story lacks its "aboutness".
**Apply it:**
- List five near-invisible qualities of the current experience - warmth, sound, residue - and pick one to make explicit.
- Rewrite the brand story's closing paragraph around that single unnameable quality the customer would feel but not report.
- In the identity deck, show two near-identical mockups and annotate the infrathin that separates them.
**Watch out:** Claiming an infrathin difference that customers can't actually perceive.
**Pairs with:** 61 Phenomenology, 75 Stickiness, 78 Super Normal

### 57. Narrative Transportation
**In one line:** Immersed audiences stop counterarguing; build the design story so the real world recedes while it runs.
**Use it when:** A skeptical panel needs convincing; a cause campaign must move readers; a product demo reads like a spec sheet.
**Apply it:**
- Open the deck with a staged scene: materials, setting, and how the user feels mid-use, before any claim.
- Give exposition just enough fact to block counterargument; surplus data pulls readers out.
- Add one strong sensory passage - sound, temperature, texture - to the narrative's middle.
**Watch out:** Under-explaining the setup, so audiences argue instead of entering.
**Pairs with:** 9 Character Relatability, 87 The Senses, 90 The Willing Suspension of Disbelief and Poetic Faith

### 68. Rhythm and Silence
**In one line:** Pace the design story with deliberate peaks, rests, and transitions so attention and comprehension hold.
**Use it when:** A dense report puts readers to sleep; a talk runs at one speed; slides lack breathing room.
**Apply it:**
- Break the report into sections with transition lines and short pauses, so each idea gets cognitive whitespace.
- Vary sentence and slide length: a long setup, then a one-line slide that lands hard.
- Sequence tension then release across the deck, and add white space around the single most important frame.
**Watch out:** Filling every gap; without silence the audience can't absorb or anticipate anything.
**Pairs with:** 54 Music, 59 Performance, 69 Rule of Three

### 75. Stickiness
**In one line:** Make the idea simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and told as a story people retell.
**Use it when:** A rebrand message keeps getting paraphrased wrongly; a campaign must travel by word of mouth; a feature list is forgettable.
**Apply it:**
- Compress the positioning to one sentence with no gaps a reteller would need to fill.
- Rewrite three abstract claims as concrete, sensory specifics a client can picture immediately.
- Audit the campaign against SUCCESs - Simplicity, Unexpectedness, Concreteness, Credibility, Emotion, Story - and fix the weakest.
**Watch out:** Trading accuracy for cleverness; a surprising claim still needs evidence.
**Pairs with:** 1 Affect as Information Theory, 16 Deceptive Cadence, 73 Show, Don't Tell

### 85. The Paradox of Suspense
**In one line:** Familiarity doesn't kill suspense; repeat audiences still lean in when fear and hope stay balanced.
**Use it when:** Rerunning a proven case study; a sequel campaign recycles a known story; stakeholders already know your ending.
**Apply it:**
- Re-pitch the familiar case study moment by moment, withholding the outcome you assume they remember.
- Write the deck so a desirable outcome feels reachable but not assured, keeping hope and fear both live.
- Add a revisit detail that reframes what came before, rewarding audiences who know the plot.
**Watch out:** Assuming a known ending means no tension; that's where suspense lives.
**Pairs with:** 16 Deceptive Cadence, 57 Narrative Transportation, 86 The Peak-End Rule

### 87. The Senses
**In one line:** Describe the design through sight, sound, touch, taste, and smell to make the story physically memorable.
**Use it when:** A visual-only deck feels flat; a service story needs texture; a product's quality is hard to show in words.
**Apply it:**
- Rewrite three product descriptions to name a sound, a texture, and a temperature instead of a superlative.
- Add a sensory callout beside the hero screenshot - how the moment feels or sounds.
- Attach a scent or sound cue to the service journey map for staff to stage.
**Watch out:** Telling only what it looks like; the least retained form of description.
**Pairs with:** 51 Metaphors, 57 Narrative Transportation, 73 Show, Don't Tell

### 90. The Willing Suspension of Disbelief and Poetic Faith
**In one line:** Ground bold claims in facts, human characters, and consistent rules so audiences choose to believe you.
**Use it when:** Pitching a concept far from today's reality; a speculative vision needs credibility; audiences are saturated with marketing claims.
**Apply it:**
- Anchor every future-state claim in the deck with a factual basis, real or carefully constructed.
- Give the product or brand persona relatable human traits so the leap feels smaller.
- Write a short rules block for the concept world and hold to it on every slide.
**Watch out:** Breaking your own established rules; one inconsistency collapses the whole belief.
**Pairs with:** 57 Narrative Transportation, 96 Verisimilitude, 100 World-Building

### 95. Transformation
**In one line:** Design the story so someone - user, audience, organization - ends visibly different from how they began.
**Use it when:** A case study must show before and after; a campaign aims to change behavior; a rebrand needs a journey.
**Apply it:**
- Structure the case study as a clear before-and-after, with one named change per stage.
- Add a closing scene showing the audience member's new behavior or belief after adopting the design.
- Rewrite the conclusion so the stakes and the payoff both shift, not just the product.
**Watch out:** Showing only the product change; missing the human transformation gives no emotion.
**Pairs with:** 12 Contrast, Beginning to End, 83 The Hero's Journey

### 98. Vulnerability
**In one line:** Show a real weakness, setback, or lesson, alongside how it was addressed, to earn audience trust.
**Use it when:** A founder presents to investors; a postmortem report needs credibility; a brand story sounds too polished to trust.
**Apply it:**
- Insert one slide naming a design flaw or failed earlier version, plus the fix now in place.
- In the About or founder page, add a candid obstacle that shaped the work.
- Reword overconfident claims to acknowledge limits while stating how each is being remediated.
**Watch out:** Confessing a fault without showing its remedy; exposure alone reads as risk.
**Pairs with:** 9 Character Relatability, 11 Conflict, 23 Empathy
