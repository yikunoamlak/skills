# Psychology + Philosophy (19 principles)

This category supplies the "why" beneath a design story: how audiences actually perceive, remember, judge, and attach meaning to what designers make. It blends cognitive science, design ethics, and market philosophy so an agent can defend a choice rather than merely describe one. Use these entries to ground narrative decisions in how people think.

### 19. Dieter Rams' Principles of Design
**In one line:** Ten tests for good design: useful, honest, understandable, long-lasting, environmentally sound, and as little design as possible.
**Use it when:** Auditing a landing page for claims; deciding if a rebrand adds or subtracts; arguing for restraint with a client wanting decoration.
**Apply it:**
- Score the artifact against all ten principles; log each as pass or fail.
- Delete claims, ornaments, and features that do not serve the core task.
- Rewrite copy so every superlative is provable and no promise outruns the product.
**Watch out:** Quoting the principles in a deck while the artifact quietly violates several.
**Pairs with:** 26 Ethics, 78 Super Normal, 29 Form and Function, 43 Kill Your Darlings

### 26. Ethics
**In one line:** Ten checks - usability, safety, accessibility, transparency, restraint in persuasion - for work that treats audiences well.
**Use it when:** A campaign nudges too hard; an accessibility check before launch; a client asks whether the story overpromises or excludes anyone.
**Apply it:**
- Add the ten ethics considerations as a checklist and answer each before release.
- Rewrite persuasion-heavy copy so claims stay transparent and reader autonomy survives.
- Audit contrast, alt text, and reading level so the story reaches diverse abilities.
**Watch out:** Reading the list as moralizing; deliver it as a quiet pre-flight check.
**Pairs with:** 35 Hofstede's Dimensions, 19 Rams' Principles, 47 Maslow's Hierarchy, 98 Vulnerability

### 35. Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions Theory
**In one line:** Six dimensions - power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, time orientation, indulgence - that predict cultural response.
**Use it when:** Localizing a campaign for a new market; a global pitch might carry unwanted hierarchy cues; choosing tone for a mixed-nationality audience.
**Apply it:**
- Rate the target culture on all six dimensions and record the extreme scores.
- Adjust pronouns, imagery, and authority figures to fit individualism and power-distance scores.
- Add proof points and process detail for high uncertainty-avoidance audiences.
**Watch out:** Stereotyping a whole country from one score; treat dimensions as tendencies, not rules.
**Pairs with:** 26 Ethics, 58 Observing, 100 World-Building, 41 Jobs to Be Done

### 36. Human Technology
**In one line:** The innate abilities - cognition, speech, senses, movement - that let people use any built technology.
**Use it when:** A product story names only the software; you must justify an accessibility feature; a UI claim needs grounding in what people can actually perceive.
**Apply it:**
- Pair every technology named in the deck with the human ability that enables it.
- Rewrite onboarding copy around perception and memory limits instead of feature lists.
- Add one line per screen naming the sense or cognitive step it relies on.
**Watch out:** Praising the tool while ignoring the user's unaided capability or limit.
**Pairs with:** 58 Observing, 76 S-O-R, 79 Synaptic Pruning, 87 The Senses

### 46. Mary's Room
**In one line:** A thought experiment showing that knowing every fact about an experience still leaves the feeling itself unlearned.
**Use it when:** A pitch is all specs and no sensation; stakeholders claim research covers the customer; you must sell the feel of a product.
**Apply it:**
- Add one qualia moment - a texture, scent, or sound described first-person.
- Convert a table of specs into a scene where a person experiences the product.
- Ask the client what customers would feel, not only what they would know.
**Watch out:** Stopping at description; facts alone leave the audience cold, exactly like Mary's Room.
**Pairs with:** 61 Phenomenology, 87 The Senses, 22 Emotion, 57 Narrative Transportation

### 49. Meaning versus Value
**In one line:** Value is measurable; meaning is symbolic and emotional. Stories need both in harmony, not contradicting each other.
**Use it when:** Pricing a beloved legacy product; a charity film risks sounding transactional; deciding whether a case study leads with metrics or emotion.
**Apply it:**
- Mark each slide as value (measurable) or meaning (personal), then rebalance the deck.
- Pair every statistic with one human scene so neither register dominates.
- Strip price and specs from emotional sections to avoid cognitive dissonance.
**Watch out:** Naming a price for something the story has just framed as priceless.
**Pairs with:** 91 Quarter-Inch Hole, 41 Jobs to Be Done, 9 Character Relatability

### 50. Mentalese
**In one line:** The language of thought hypothesis: ideas form before words, in pre-verbal images and intentions.
**Use it when:** Sketching before naming a concept; a client fixates on wording too early; unblocking a team stuck in terminology debates.
**Apply it:**
- Open the workshop with image or mood-board prompts before anyone writes a headline.
- Keep the first concept pass word-free: thumbnails, sketches, and references only.
- Translate rough visuals into copy last, letting audience inference fill the gaps.
**Watch out:** Naming too early; the chosen words freeze the idea before it develops.
**Pairs with:** 77 Stream of Consciousness, 3 Analogy, 73 Show, Don't Tell, 14 Creativity

### 56. Narrative Intelligence
**In one line:** The storytelling counterpart of emotional intelligence: a practiced ability to hear, judge, and craft narratives.
**Use it when:** Pitch rehearsals keep falling flat; you must brief a team on story craft; you need to know whether a client presentation holds attention.
**Apply it:**
- Run monthly story sessions where designers tell and critique work before client reveal.
- Log audience reactions - bored or engaged - after each pitch, then adjust.
- Collect stories from varied genres and people, noting what made each land.
**Watch out:** Practicing delivery without listening; NI grows from observing other storytellers too.
**Pairs with:** 50 Mentalese, 21 Dramaturgy, 57 Narrative Transportation, 59 Performance

### 58. Observing
**In one line:** Watch real people in their own setting and turn what they actually do into second-person narrative.
**Use it when:** Surveys say users love a feature they never touch; you need evidence for a client; research must reveal unspoken behavior.
**Apply it:**
- Observe four or five users at home or work, recording behavior with permission.
- Rewrite shorthand notes as second-person narrative so the client stands in the user's shoes.
- Rate each change in affect from mild to strong and quote the strongest.
**Watch out:** Counting behaviors from a survey; frequency never explains why someone misplaces the remote.
**Pairs with:** 61 Phenomenology, 60 Personas, 70 Sensemaking, 13 Core Values

### 61. Phenomenology
**In one line:** Study how things seem to a conscious subject - qualia and intentionality - rather than how they measure.
**Use it when:** A specification-heavy report misses how it feels; sensory branding needs words; a story must separate appearance from actuality.
**Apply it:**
- Write how the artifact seems - light, sound, weight - alongside what it is.
- Give every scene a directed object: name exactly what consciousness points at.
- Flag which claims describe illusion and which describe reality, and label them honestly.
**Watch out:** Describing measurements only; objective detail never conveys what an experience is like.
**Pairs with:** 46 Mary's Room, 87 The Senses, 96 Verisimilitude, 57 Narrative Transportation

### 70. Sensemaking
**In one line:** Weick's account of how people build plausible interpretations from identity, contextual cues, and social influence, continuously.
**Use it when:** A rebrand confuses existing customers; the deck presents data without a through-line; stakeholders invent their own explanation of the change.
**Apply it:**
- Anchor the story in cues the audience already trusts, then link them into one frame.
- Present interpretations as retrospective and plausible, admitting openly what changed your mind.
- End with one map that moves the audience from anxiety to action.
**Watch out:** Optimizing for accuracy when audiences only need a plausible story that makes sense.
**Pairs with:** 17 Deductions, 61 Phenomenology, 76 S-O-R, 55 Narrative Design

### 74. Star Trek
**In one line:** Fiction and reality feed each other: a believable imagined future can mobilize people to build it.
**Use it when:** A vision statement feels abstract; the category does not exist yet; you must rally a team around an unfinished future.
**Apply it:**
- Open with a specific, credible scene of the future the product makes ordinary.
- Cast a fictional persona or scenario as the emblem a team can rally behind.
- Name one real milestone the vision already inspired, then project the next.
**Watch out:** Selling pure fantasy; audiences believe only futures that rhyme with present evidence.
**Pairs with:** 6 Aspiration, 100 World-Building, 33 Happily Ever After, 95 Transformation

### 76. Stimulus, Organism, Response Theory (S-O-R)
**In one line:** A stimulus is evaluated by the organism before any response; identical designs provoke different reactions because of inner history.
**Use it when:** Two markets react oppositely to the same ad; a color choice triggers unexpected pushback; you must pre-empt a visceral objection.
**Apply it:**
- List likely visceral responses to each color, image, and texture, positive and negative.
- Add context lines that let the audience respond consciously instead of reflexively.
- Pair every strong stimulus with a reassuring descriptor that reframes it.
**Watch out:** Assuming one intended reaction; past experience, not your intent, drives the response.
**Pairs with:** 1 Affect as Information, 22 Emotion, 47 Maslow's Hierarchy, 87 The Senses

### 77. Stream of Consciousness
**In one line:** The unpunctuated flow of a person's thoughts, captured verbatim to show how they think, not just what.
**Use it when:** Usability transcripts sit unused; you need one authentic voice in a report; a story reads too tidy and scripted.
**Apply it:**
- Run think-aloud testing, asking participants to narrate feeling, action, and thought without stopping or editing.
- Paste verbatim first-person fragments from the session into the report as pull quotes.
- Represent every participant's viewpoint if the story claims multiple perspectives, not just one.
**Watch out:** Tidying quotes into neat sentences; once edited, the rawness stops being evidence.
**Pairs with:** 50 Mentalese, 60 Personas, 23 Empathy, 58 Observing

### 78. Super Normal
**In one line:** Fukasawa and Morrison's case for design that conceals itself - ordinary, quiet, memorable through absence.
**Use it when:** A client wants flashier visuals; luxury cues feel wrong for a utility brand; you must defend a stripped-back identity to a skeptical room.
**Apply it:**
- Remove ornament that does not improve the core task, then show before and after.
- Write the pitch around what the design deliberately leaves out.
- Test whether the offering still works described only by its basic purpose.
**Watch out:** Confusing ordinary with boring; super normal is restraint, not neglect.
**Pairs with:** 19 Rams' Principles, 29 Form and Function, 39 Infrathin

### 79. Synaptic Pruning
**In one line:** The brain keeps only connections it uses, so audiences lose capacity for ideas they were never allowed to practice.
**Use it when:** An audience dismisses an unfamiliar approach; a team insists it cannot draw or tell stories; innovation meets inherited habit.
**Apply it:**
- Add a primer slide that exercises the unfamiliar idea before you argue for it.
- Rehearse the new concept in small doses across the deck rather than once.
- Reframe resistance as unfamiliarity, then supply the practice that rebuilds the connection.
**Watch out:** Reading rejection as indifference; those synapses may simply never have been exercised.
**Pairs with:** 36 Human Technology, 56 Narrative Intelligence, 69 Rule of Three

### 86. The Peak-End Rule
**In one line:** People remember the most intense moment and the ending, not the duration or detail of an experience.
**Use it when:** A long case study sags in the middle; a critique ends on criticism; onboarding flows bury the payoff.
**Apply it:**
- Place the single most emotional moment at the deck's high point, roughly two-thirds through.
- Rewrite the closing slide so it ends on relief or gain, even after bad news.
- Cut middling sections the narrating self would discard anyway.
**Watch out:** Saving all effort for the finale while the middle stays flat.
**Pairs with:** 30 Freytag's Pyramid, 62 Pixar's Story Structure, 22 Emotion

### 91. Theodore Levitt and the Quarter-Inch Hole
**In one line:** Customers hire outcomes, not objects; tell the story of the hole, not the drill.
**Use it when:** Feature sheets dominate the proposal; naming the category limits growth; a client debates specifications instead of results.
**Apply it:**
- Strip the deck of screens, components, and journey maps; describe only the human outcome.
- Rewrite the headline as the job the customer is hiring the product to do.
- Rename the company or category around the benefit, avoiding marketing myopia.
**Watch out:** Describing the drill in finer detail; specification talk deepens marketing myopia.
**Pairs with:** 41 Jobs to Be Done, 49 Meaning versus Value, 99 Work Backwards

### 92. Thesis + Antithesis = Synthesis
**In one line:** State a proposition, negate it, then reconcile both into a stronger new proposal.
**Use it when:** Explaining how a design was arrived at; two stakeholders deadlock on opposite options; a concept needs visible vetting.
**Apply it:**
- Label three slides thesis, antithesis, synthesis, one proposition per slide.
- Assign roles: junior introduces the thesis, a second designer the antithesis, a senior the synthesis.
- Close with the reconciled proposal and the truths kept from both sides.
**Watch out:** Skipping the negation; without genuine conflict, synthesis is just the first idea restated.
**Pairs with:** 11 Conflict, 43 Kill Your Darlings, 32 Gestalt, 89 The Wall
