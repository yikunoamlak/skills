# Methods / Tools (21 principles)

This category holds the working apparatus behind a design story: frameworks for finding material, structuring it, testing it, and delivering it to a room. Use these entries to move from raw inputs to a narrative an audience can follow, verify, and act on.

### 13. Core Values
**In one line:** State the beliefs guiding the team so audiences judge the work by what it stands for.
**Use it when:** A pitch must show what you won't compromise on; a rebrand needs a stated ethic; a client asks why this team.
**Apply it:**
- Draft three to five short values a designer could repeat from memory in a meeting.
- Back each value with one project decision or hiring rule that visibly follows it.
- Add a values section to the deck, then cut any value the studio routinely breaks.
**Watch out:** Values nobody applies; unbacked slogans read as hypocrisy and discount everything else.
**Pairs with:** 53 Mission, Vision, Value Statements, 67 Resource-Based Theory, 26 Ethics

### 14. Creativity
**In one line:** Treat imagination as a trainable input; storytelling reopens the idea-generating ability habit has narrowed.
**Use it when:** A brainstorm stalls; a client calls the category boring; a data-heavy deck needs a hook nobody has seen.
**Apply it:**
- Score each concept on originality, usefulness, and surprise before it enters the pitch deck.
- Run a story round where every idea must beat two of the three criteria to stay.
- Prototype one deliberately odd variant and pitch it beside the safe option to show range.
**Watch out:** Novelty without utility; an original idea nobody can use dies in review.
**Pairs with:** 15 Data Storytelling, 65 Prototyping, 69 Rule of Three

### 15. Data Storytelling
**In one line:** Turn isolated numbers into a pattern the audience can read, trust, and act on.
**Use it when:** A report must move a decision; stakeholders dispute the chart; analytics need explaining to non-technical clients.
**Apply it:**
- Rebuild each chart around one audience question; strip gridlines, labels, and series it does not need.
- Annotate the anomaly, its benchmark, and the period it should be read against.
- State the required action below the exhibit, using all three axes X, Y, and Z.
**Watch out:** Decorative chart junk and extra series obscure the point faster than bad data.
**Pairs with:** 17 Deductions, 41 Jobs to Be Done, 7 Calls to Action

### 17. Deductions
**In one line:** Supply the clues that let an audience reach your intended conclusion instead of a wrong one.
**Use it when:** A case study skips steps; a proposal leaves readers guessing; a claim keeps drawing skeptical pushback.
**Apply it:**
- Play the deck aloud and list every inferential jump; insert the missing premise before each conclusion.
- Order evidence general to specific so each slide narrows toward the decision you want.
- Test each claim against a counterexample and note where it would fail.
**Watch out:** Assuming shared knowledge; audiences fill gaps with their own wrong deduction.
**Pairs with:** 21 Dramaturgy, 55 Narrative Design, 73 Show, Don't Tell

### 21. Dramaturgy
**In one line:** Adapt a design narrative into a performable structure through research, testing, and managed impressions.
**Use it when:** A complex service must be explained simply; a pitch must survive a hostile room; a launch spans many touchpoints.
**Apply it:**
- Map the total experience from first awareness to post-purchase, marking every moment the story is told.
- Audit the sign vehicles settings, appearance, and manners of interacting against the identity the brand wants read.
- Simplify technical or archaic detail the way a dramaturge clarifies dense dialogue for actors.
**Watch out:** Staging polish without substance; a hollow experience shows faster under good impression management.
**Pairs with:** 10 Choreography, 5 Archetypes, 59 Performance

### 32. Gestalt
**In one line:** Design the whole before the parts, since audiences perceive the composite before they dissect it.
**Use it when:** A landing page feels disjointed; a brand system needs coherence; a slide deck confuses rather than clarifies.
**Apply it:**
- Review the layout against closure, similarity, continuation, proximity, common fate, figure-ground, and Prägnanz.
- Regroup scattered elements by similarity or proximity so the eye forms your intended clusters.
- Keep the background quieter than the figure so the hero element reads first.
- Blur the screen; if the message still reads, the gestalt is holding.
**Watch out:** Tuning parts in isolation; fine elements can still assemble into a confusing whole.
**Pairs with:** 66 Proximity, 29 Form and Function, 64 Presentations

### 33. Happily Ever After
**In one line:** Write the chapter after delivery, describing how the design keeps living, aging, and being updated.
**Use it when:** A client asks what happens post-launch; a product nears end of relevance; a proposal needs a maintenance story.
**Apply it:**
- Append a closing slide projecting usage, peak relevance, and the first required update of the design.
- List human needs that appear only after adoption, then design the follow-on service or release.
- Put the date the design stops working into the narrative with a plan behind it.
**Watch out:** Treating handoff as an ending; unaddressed post-launch needs become churn and complaints.
**Pairs with:** 41 Jobs to Be Done, 95 Transformation, 99 Work Backwards

### 34. Hitchcock's Rules of Visual Storytelling
**In one line:** Open wide to orient, then scale every element to match its importance in the argument.
**Use it when:** A hero image says too much; a case study buries its subject; a deck opens on minor detail.
**Apply it:**
- Open the deck with one establishing visual or statement before any characters or features appear.
- Frame each view from the audience's position so the eye lands where the argument needs it.
- Enlarge the single decisive element and shrink supporting elements to match its narrative weight.
**Watch out:** Sizing everything equally; the audience cannot tell what matters.
**Pairs with:** 73 Show, Don't Tell, 52 Mise en Scène, 64 Presentations

### 40. Jekyll and Hyde Syndrome
**In one line:** One brand behaving two ways across channels reads as inauthentic and makes people abandon it.
**Use it when:** Social tone clashes with the site; a franchise feels premium online and cheap in person; two merged voices never reconciled.
**Apply it:**
- Audit every channel's copy, imagery, and service script for tonal and factual contradictions.
- Write one shared voice guide with do and don't examples, then rewrite the worst page first.
- Place the two most divergent touchpoints side by side and reconcile them line by line.
**Watch out:** Fixing visuals while leaving staff behavior inconsistent; the mismatch returns at the counter.
**Pairs with:** 5 Archetypes, 11 Conflict, 94 Tone and Tenor

### 41. Jobs to Be Done
**In one line:** State the job a person hires your design for, not the features it happens to have.
**Use it when:** Positioning drifts into specs; a launch needs one sharp sentence; research returns wish lists instead of motives.
**Apply it:**
- Write the statement: I need to, when, so that, without, then verify it in research.
- Rewrite the landing headline around the job outcome rather than the product category.
- Cut from the story any feature that serves neither the primary job nor its emotional payoff.
**Watch out:** Describing the product instead of the job; you then compete on features you may lose.
**Pairs with:** 91 Theodore Levitt, 42 Kano and Storytelling, 60 Personas

### 53. Mission/Vision/Value Statements
**In one line:** Three short statements fix what is, what will be, and the value promised.
**Use it when:** A studio has no North Star copy; a deck needs an elevator pitch; a rebrand requires aligned language.
**Apply it:**
- Answer four questions: purpose, future improvement, unique value, and why you are qualified to deliver it.
- List action words describing the organization, keep the strongest three to five, and draft from them.
- Test each statement with internal and external audiences, then cut whatever fails either.
**Watch out:** Verbosity and vagueness; a mission nobody can repeat is not a mission.
**Pairs with:** 13 Core Values, 7 Calls to Action, 45 Lexicon

### 54. Music
**In one line:** Compose the narrative in measures, letting words and scenes carry rhythm, harmony, and a memorable theme.
**Use it when:** A talk drags at one even pace; a case study lacks a hook; a deck repeats a flat cadence.
**Apply it:**
- Divide the deck into equal sections and assign each a beat: setup, escalation, payoff.
- Compose a two-line theme phrase restating the core message at every section break.
- Vary sentence and slide length deliberately, inserting a blank slide where attention should reset.
**Watch out:** Complexity mistaken for quality; extra notes and slides dilute the melody.
**Pairs with:** 68 Rhythm and Silence, 32 Gestalt, 69 Rule of Three

### 59. Performance
**In one line:** Tell rather than act; keep the exchange interactive and let the design stay the star.
**Use it when:** A presenter leans on jokes; stage fright looms; a pitch has become a monotone monologue.
**Apply it:**
- Add three audience questions with short answers and mark one pause per section of the script.
- Insert a personal anecdote about what inspired the design and its effect on the team.
- End with a call to action that opens a dialogue rather than a curtain call.
**Watch out:** Performing instead of connecting; spectacle makes people remember the presenter, not the work.
**Pairs with:** 64 Presentations, 21 Dramaturgy, 7 Calls to Action

### 64. Presentations
**In one line:** Give each slide one idea, one minute, five to seven elements, and one takeaway.
**Use it when:** A deck is bloated with bullets; the meeting is short; the talk must work without slides.
**Apply it:**
- Split any slide carrying more than one idea into separate slides with informative headings.
- Remove decorative images, keep only visuals that aid understanding, and cap elements at seven.
- Rewrite each slide so it restates a key takeaway and ends with a clear call to action.
**Watch out:** Reading bullets aloud; the deck should enhance the message, not replace the speaker.
**Pairs with:** 59 Performance, 32 Gestalt, 21 Dramaturgy

### 65. Prototyping
**In one line:** Build a rough artifact of the unrealized design so audiences can touch the story and question it live.
**Use it when:** Drawings fail to convince stakeholders; the concept is a radical departure; a workshop needs participant interaction.
**Apply it:**
- Produce a foam core, wireframe, or napkin-scale model covering the whole user journey.
- Script a walkthrough narrating how customers and staff move through the prototype.
- Capture every question raised in the session and turn each into a revision or slide.
**Watch out:** Overbuilding fidelity; a prototype too finished draws critique of polish instead of concept.
**Pairs with:** 14 Creativity, 25 Essence Model, 48 Master Planning

### 66. Proximity
**In one line:** Place related elements and related claims close together so readers group them and feel the connection.
**Use it when:** A layout's sections read as unrelated; a service story splits hardware from support; local relevance matters to the room.
**Apply it:**
- Cluster related components with tighter spacing and separate groups with larger gaps.
- Name a nearby landmark or neighbor to locate the offering in the audience's world.
- Move service, hardware, and support claims into one section instead of scattering them.
**Watch out:** Arbitrary closeness; unrelated items placed together imply a relationship that does not exist.
**Pairs with:** 32 Gestalt, 47 Maslow's Hierarchy of Need, 100 World-Building

### 67. Resource-Based Theory (Marketing)
**In one line:** Argue advantage from what is valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and impossible to substitute.
**Use it when:** A pitch must beat competitors; a studio writes its capability statement; a proposal defends a premium fee.
**Apply it:**
- Inventory patents, methods, reputation, and people, rating each on the four tenets.
- Build a slide contrasting your two strongest tenets against named competitors.
- Tie every claimed advantage to a client outcome so rarity reads as value.
- Note core values and design management practices that make the advantage repeatable across projects.
**Watch out:** Listing resources nobody can verify; unproven rarity reads as boasting.
**Pairs with:** 13 Core Values, 21 Dramaturgy, 49 Meaning versus Value

### 82. The Five Ps
**In one line:** Cover product, price, promotion, place, and people so no commercial question goes unanswered.
**Use it when:** A sales-ready pitch is coming; a client asks about cost and channels; the story fixates on the product.
**Apply it:**
- Add a price line with an approximate or comparable figure so the question is never dodged.
- Add a place section covering where the design is bought, serviced, and actually used.
- Add a people slide naming designers, investors, support staff, and the users themselves.
**Watch out:** Leading with product and dropping people; audiences notice who is missing from the story.
**Pairs with:** 40 Jekyll and Hyde Syndrome, 7 Calls to Action, 35 Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions Theory

### 84. The MacGuffin
**In one line:** Name an intangible that starts the plot but whose contents nobody needs to see.
**Use it when:** A case study lacks momentum; a dry subject needs curiosity; an unseen team or asset should still be felt.
**Apply it:**
- Surface one intangible a patent, license, or method early as the thing driving events.
- Mention an absent contributor by name and one trait, withholding detail to build curiosity.
- Remove any MacGuffin detail that does not accelerate the plot toward the decision.
**Watch out:** Explaining the MacGuffin; detailing its contents drains the momentum it created.
**Pairs with:** 63 Plot, 28 Foreshadowing, 88 The Trigger or Wicked Problem

### 99. Work Backwards
**In one line:** Draft from the known ending so the opening carries only the clues the ending needs.
**Use it when:** The outcome is already approved; exposition bloats the intro; a proposal's opening fails to hook.
**Apply it:**
- Write the final slide first: the delivered result, its value, and the audience's decision.
- Work backward to list secondary stakeholders and objectives the forward draft never considered.
- Cut exposition until every opening detail is required by the ending you wrote.
**Watch out:** Reversing the telling instead of the planning; audiences still need forward order.
**Pairs with:** 27 Exposition, 72 Seven-Point Story Structure, 33 Happily Ever After

### 100. World-Building
**In one line:** Describe the context, resources, people, and rules around the design so it has somewhere to live.
**Use it when:** A concept floats without context; a service spans many actors; a culture-sensitive launch needs grounding.
**Apply it:**
- Build a world sheet covering context, resources, economics, history, politics, and technology for the design.
- Populate it with diverse personas, rituals, and language, then reuse those details across touchpoints.
- Add an education and support path showing how users learn the design and where they get help.
**Watch out:** Dumping lore; a world overloaded with detail buries the design it framed.
**Pairs with:** 48 Master Planning, 8 Ceremonies and Rituals, 25 Essence Model
