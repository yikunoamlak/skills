# The 100 Principles - Quick Index

Routing table. Scan this first; open a category file only for the entries you will actually use.
Full guidance per principle lives in the five category files in this folder.

Principle names and categories follow *Universal Principles of Storytelling for Designers: 100 Key Concepts for Bringing Good Ideas to Life*, Lyle H. Sandler (Rockport Books). The one-line summaries are original.

## Literary Device (24) - `references/literary-device.md`

- **2. Allegory** - A story whose surface events carry a second, hidden meaning about something else.
- **3. Analogy (Design by Analogy, DbA)** - Comparing two unlike things so the unfamiliar one becomes instantly clear from the familiar one.
- **4. Anthropomorphism** - Giving human traits like a face, voice, or feelings to machines, products, and services.
- **5. Archetypes** - Universal character patterns like the Rebel or Caregiver that instantly signal a design's personality.
- **7. Calls to Action** - A clear invitation that tells the audience exactly what to do next, and why.
- **11. Conflict** - The friction between opposing forces that gives a design story stakes and momentum.
- **18. Deus ex Machina** - An unexpected outside force resolves a problem the characters could never solve themselves.
- **24. Epigraph** - A short quotation placed before the story to set tone, theme, or expectation.
- **27. Exposition** - The background information an audience needs before the story can make sense.
- **28. Foreshadowing** - Hinting that something important is coming, so later reveals land as planned payoffs.
- **37. Hyperbole** - Deliberate exaggeration that makes a mundane point vivid without being taken literally.
- **38. In Medias Res** - Starting the story in the middle of the action, then working backward to context.
- **43. Kill Your Darlings** - Removing favored content and features that don't serve the story or the design.
- **45. Lexicon** - A curated list of words that describe your design and sound right to its audience.
- **51. Metaphors** - A figure of speech that states one thing is another to make an abstract idea concrete.
- **52. Mise en Scène** - Staging the environment, props, lighting, and people around a design the way a scene is set.
- **60. Personas** - Fictional characters built from observed real users who stand in for your audience.
- **63. Plot** - The chain of causes and effects that moves a story from setup to resolution.
- **73. Show, Don't Tell** - Lead with vivid sensory detail instead of flat factual claims, so audiences feel the design.
- **80. Synecdoche** - Using a part to stand for the whole, or the whole for one part.
- **88. The Trigger or Wicked Problem** - The underlying why behind a project: the messy, high-stakes problem that makes design necessary.
- **89. The Wall** - The invisible barrier between storyteller and audience; breaking it means addressing them directly.
- **94. Tone and Tenor** - Tone is the mood of a message; tenor is the constant subject beneath it.
- **96. Verisimilitude** - The appearance of being true: enough realism that an audience accepts the story as credible.

## Structure (21) - `references/structure.md`

- **8. Ceremonies and Rituals** - Shared rituals - critique, stand-ups, unboxing - focus attention, bind people, and seed a design story with repeatable meaning.
- **10. Choreography** - Sequence and stage every interaction - click, pause, path - so the design's flow reads like choreographed movement.
- **12. Contrast, Beginning to End** - Show a visible gap between the ordinary world before your design and the changed reality after it.
- **20. Disney's Twelve Basic Principles of Animation** - Twelve animation fundamentals - anticipation, staging, timing, appeal - that make interfaces, decks, and motion feel alive.
- **25. Essence Model** - Define a design's essence through four lenses: observable attributes, intent, how it makes users appear, how it feels.
- **29. Form and Function** - Balance what a design looks like with what it does; the narrative has to prove both.
- **30. Freytag's Pyramid** - A five-stage arc - exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement - built to release catharsis.
- **31. Functions of Storytelling** - Stories work several jobs at once - entertain, teach, warn, explain, preserve culture, persuade - so choose yours.
- **42. Kano and Storytelling** - Sort features into basics, performance, and WOW tiers to decide which part of the product earns the story.
- **44. Kishōtenketsu** - A four-act structure - introduction, development, twist, reconciliation - that builds meaning without conflict.
- **47. Maslow's Hierarchy of Need** - Map the design onto five levels of human need, from essential function up to self-actualization.
- **48. Master Planning** - Read a brand or service as a city with six places: marketplace, park, temple, school, stage, neighborhoods.
- **55. Narrative Design** - Write the whole experience as plain text, step by step, before building any high-fidelity prototype.
- **62. Pixar's Story Structure** - Fill a five-line story spine - ordinary world, disruption, consequences, resolution - to draft any narrative fast.
- **69. Rule of Three** - Group ideas in threes - the smallest number that reads as a pattern - and put the strongest last.
- **71. Seven Basic Plots** - Match the design challenge to one of seven classic plots to give it a familiar, resonant shape.
- **72. Seven-Point Story Structure** - Run the seven-point structure backward - start at the resolution and work toward exposition to generate ideas.
- **81. The Fichtean Curve** - Skip the setup and open on a stack of escalating crises, saving exposition for later.
- **83. The Hero's Journey** - Follow the hero from ordinary world through call, mentor, ordeal, and return with a hard-won solution.
- **93. Three-Act Structure** - Split the story into setup, struggle, and resolution, each anchored by at least one clear story beat.
- **97. Vonnegut's Shapes of Stories** - Plot a story's fortunes on good-bad versus time to reveal its shape, then pick a shape for the design.

## Emotion (15) - `references/emotion.md`

- **1. Affect as Information Theory** - Treat emotion as usable evidence in a decision, not decoration on top of rational facts.
- **6. Aspiration** - Frame the design as the vehicle that carries an audience toward a goal they already want.
- **9. Character Relatability** - Give every character motives, flaws, and an origin so audiences connect with the design story.
- **16. Deceptive Cadence** - Withhold a key reveal, then break the expected pattern to recapture attention at the close.
- **22. Emotion** - Aim every design story at a specific feeling, then give the audience somewhere to act on it.
- **23. Empathy** - Move from knowing audience data to understanding the people behind it, then design from that understanding.
- **39. Infrathin** - Name the barely-perceptible difference that makes one design feel unlike an otherwise identical rival.
- **57. Narrative Transportation** - Immersed audiences stop counterarguing; build the design story so the real world recedes while it runs.
- **68. Rhythm and Silence** - Pace the design story with deliberate peaks, rests, and transitions so attention and comprehension hold.
- **75. Stickiness** - Make the idea simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and told as a story people retell.
- **85. The Paradox of Suspense** - Familiarity doesn't kill suspense; repeat audiences still lean in when fear and hope stay balanced.
- **87. The Senses** - Describe the design through sight, sound, touch, taste, and smell to make the story physically memorable.
- **90. The Willing Suspension of Disbelief and Poetic Faith** - Ground bold claims in facts, human characters, and consistent rules so audiences choose to believe you.
- **95. Transformation** - Design the story so someone - user, audience, organization - ends visibly different from how they began.
- **98. Vulnerability** - Show a real weakness, setback, or lesson, alongside how it was addressed, to earn audience trust.

## Psychology + Philosophy (19) - `references/psychology-philosophy.md`

- **19. Dieter Rams' Principles of Design** - Ten tests for good design: useful, honest, understandable, long-lasting, environmentally sound, and as little design as possible.
- **26. Ethics** - Ten checks - usability, safety, accessibility, transparency, restraint in persuasion - for work that treats audiences well.
- **35. Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions Theory** - Six dimensions - power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, time orientation, indulgence - that predict cultural response.
- **36. Human Technology** - The innate abilities - cognition, speech, senses, movement - that let people use any built technology.
- **46. Mary's Room** - A thought experiment showing that knowing every fact about an experience still leaves the feeling itself unlearned.
- **49. Meaning versus Value** - Value is measurable; meaning is symbolic and emotional. Stories need both in harmony, not contradicting each other.
- **50. Mentalese** - The language of thought hypothesis: ideas form before words, in pre-verbal images and intentions.
- **56. Narrative Intelligence** - The storytelling counterpart of emotional intelligence: a practiced ability to hear, judge, and craft narratives.
- **58. Observing** - Watch real people in their own setting and turn what they actually do into second-person narrative.
- **61. Phenomenology** - Study how things seem to a conscious subject - qualia and intentionality - rather than how they measure.
- **70. Sensemaking** - Weick's account of how people build plausible interpretations from identity, contextual cues, and social influence, continuously.
- **74. Star Trek** - Fiction and reality feed each other: a believable imagined future can mobilize people to build it.
- **76. Stimulus, Organism, Response Theory (S-O-R)** - A stimulus is evaluated by the organism before any response; identical designs provoke different reactions because of inner history.
- **77. Stream of Consciousness** - The unpunctuated flow of a person's thoughts, captured verbatim to show how they think, not just what.
- **78. Super Normal** - Fukasawa and Morrison's case for design that conceals itself - ordinary, quiet, memorable through absence.
- **79. Synaptic Pruning** - The brain keeps only connections it uses, so audiences lose capacity for ideas they were never allowed to practice.
- **86. The Peak-End Rule** - People remember the most intense moment and the ending, not the duration or detail of an experience.
- **91. Theodore Levitt and the Quarter-Inch Hole** - Customers hire outcomes, not objects; tell the story of the hole, not the drill.
- **92. Thesis + Antithesis = Synthesis** - State a proposition, negate it, then reconcile both into a stronger new proposal.

## Methods / Tools (21) - `references/methods-tools.md`

- **13. Core Values** - State the beliefs guiding the team so audiences judge the work by what it stands for.
- **14. Creativity** - Treat imagination as a trainable input; storytelling reopens the idea-generating ability habit has narrowed.
- **15. Data Storytelling** - Turn isolated numbers into a pattern the audience can read, trust, and act on.
- **17. Deductions** - Supply the clues that let an audience reach your intended conclusion instead of a wrong one.
- **21. Dramaturgy** - Adapt a design narrative into a performable structure through research, testing, and managed impressions.
- **32. Gestalt** - Design the whole before the parts, since audiences perceive the composite before they dissect it.
- **33. Happily Ever After** - Write the chapter after delivery, describing how the design keeps living, aging, and being updated.
- **34. Hitchcock's Rules of Visual Storytelling** - Open wide to orient, then scale every element to match its importance in the argument.
- **40. Jekyll and Hyde Syndrome** - One brand behaving two ways across channels reads as inauthentic and makes people abandon it.
- **41. Jobs to Be Done** - State the job a person hires your design for, not the features it happens to have.
- **53. Mission/Vision/Value Statements** - Three short statements fix what is, what will be, and the value promised.
- **54. Music** - Compose the narrative in measures, letting words and scenes carry rhythm, harmony, and a memorable theme.
- **59. Performance** - Tell rather than act; keep the exchange interactive and let the design stay the star.
- **64. Presentations** - Give each slide one idea, one minute, five to seven elements, and one takeaway.
- **65. Prototyping** - Build a rough artifact of the unrealized design so audiences can touch the story and question it live.
- **66. Proximity** - Place related elements and related claims close together so readers group them and feel the connection.
- **67. Resource-Based Theory (Marketing)** - Argue advantage from what is valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and impossible to substitute.
- **82. The Five Ps** - Cover product, price, promotion, place, and people so no commercial question goes unanswered.
- **84. The MacGuffin** - Name an intangible that starts the plot but whose contents nobody needs to see.
- **99. Work Backwards** - Draft from the known ending so the opening carries only the clues the ending needs.
- **100. World-Building** - Describe the context, resources, people, and rules around the design so it has somewhere to live.
