# Literary Device (24 principles)

Literary devices are the toolbox designers borrow from fiction to make a pitch, page, or service story actually land. This category covers the moves that hide and reveal meaning, set audience expectations, and control how a design narrative reads. Each entry below turns a classic device into a concrete edit you can make to a client artifact.

### 2. Allegory
**In one line:** A story whose surface events carry a second, hidden meaning about something else.
**Use it when:** A rebrand must cast the old identity as a villain; a complex service model needs a parallel tale; a campaign wants readable subtext for launch.
**Apply it:**
- Map the real design story onto a parallel fictional storyline with matching beats.
- Plant a consistent, subtle clue at each stage pointing back to the real subject.
- Close by naming the behavior, risk, or value the surface story warns about.
**Watch out:** If the allegory needs decoding, readers disengage; keep a thread of literal truth.
**Pairs with:** 5 Archetypes, 51 Metaphors, 3 Analogy

### 3. Analogy (Design by Analogy, DbA)
**In one line:** Comparing two unlike things so the unfamiliar one becomes instantly clear from the familiar one.
**Use it when:** A client can't grasp an abstract platform; a pitch needs a one-sentence origin story; a new product name needs instant context for buyers.
**Apply it:**
- Pick a well-known source domain that shares structure with the design, like Velcro and burrs.
- Write the comparison as an 'X is to Y as A is to B' line for the deck.
- Check the analogy across the audience's culture and language before publishing it.
**Watch out:** An analogy that only works for you clarifies nothing; test it on an outsider.
**Pairs with:** 51 Metaphors, 80 Synecdoche, 2 Allegory

### 4. Anthropomorphism
**In one line:** Giving human traits like a face, voice, or feelings to machines, products, and services.
**Use it when:** An error state reads cold and blaming; a service robot needs personality; a hardware product wants warmth without a full rebuild.
**Apply it:**
- Rewrite system messages in first person: 'I am not feeling well' instead of code text.
- Add minimal facial cues (two dots for eyes) to a hardware or app icon.
- Write the product's service rating or recovery copy as if the product cares.
**Watch out:** Overdoing it reads as childish or manipulative; hint at humanity, don't costume the product.
**Pairs with:** 5 Archetypes, 60 Personas, 9 Character Relatability

### 5. Archetypes
**In one line:** Universal character patterns like the Rebel or Caregiver that instantly signal a design's personality.
**Use it when:** A brand strategy deck needs a one-word personality; a UI has no voice; stakeholders argue about who the product is for.
**Apply it:**
- Assign one of twelve archetypes (Rebel, Creator, Explorer) in the brand brief header.
- Rewrite the voice guidelines so copy, color, and imagery all express that archetype.
- Audit the brands competing with you and name the archetype gap your design can occupy.
**Watch out:** Choosing an archetype that contradicts actual behavior makes the brand read as inauthentic.
**Pairs with:** 9 Character Relatability, 60 Personas, 25 Essence Model

### 7. Calls to Action
**In one line:** A clear invitation that tells the audience exactly what to do next, and why.
**Use it when:** A landing page converts poorly; a pitch must end with a specific ask; wayfinding in a venue needs to move crowds.
**Apply it:**
- Replace vague buttons with a strong command verb plus the value on the other side.
- Write CTA copy in first person, such as 'Start my free trial,' for trust.
- Strip any surrounding elements that compete with the single primary request.
**Watch out:** Too aggressive pushes audiences to disengage; too passive gets ignored entirely.
**Pairs with:** 99 Work Backwards, 64 Presentations, 86 The Peak-End Rule

### 11. Conflict
**In one line:** The friction between opposing forces that gives a design story stakes and momentum.
**Use it when:** A case study feels flat; a pitch needs a villain to defeat; a service journey must show before-and-after tension for users.
**Apply it:**
- Name the two opposing forces on slide one: user versus clutter, or old way versus new.
- Choose a conflict type (character vs. technology, vs. self) and keep it consistent throughout.
- Escalate the friction through the middle of the deck before revealing the solution.
**Watch out:** Conflict with no path to resolution leaves audiences anxious rather than engaged.
**Pairs with:** 83 The Hero's Journey, 30 Freytag's Pyramid, 93 Three-Act Structure

### 18. Deus ex Machina
**In one line:** An unexpected outside force resolves a problem the characters could never solve themselves.
**Use it when:** Showing how design rescued a wicked problem; a turnaround story needs a pivotal moment; a pitch must dramatize a breakthrough result.
**Apply it:**
- Build the worst-case scenario first, describing the affliction in concrete, vivid detail.
- Reveal the designer's decisive move as the turning point of the story, then show the evidence.
- Follow immediately with proof: data, a testimonial, or a working prototype.
**Watch out:** Presenting the fix without showing the work reads as a cheap trick.
**Pairs with:** 11 Conflict, 83 The Hero's Journey, 21 Dramaturgy, 93 Three-Act Structure

### 24. Epigraph
**In one line:** A short quotation placed before the story to set tone, theme, or expectation.
**Use it when:** Opening a pitch deck with a line that frames the argument; a report needs gravitas; a keynote wants to signal the theme.
**Apply it:**
- Place one carefully sourced quote on the title slide, before any content appears.
- Choose a quote about change or craft, not one that merely names your industry or field.
- Say aloud how the quote connects when that link isn't obvious immediately.
**Watch out:** An epigraph standing in for exposition leaves the audience unoriented and impatient.
**Pairs with:** 63 Plot, 70 Sensemaking, 68 Rhythm and Silence

### 27. Exposition
**In one line:** The background information an audience needs before the story can make sense.
**Use it when:** A deck opens with features before context; clients lack industry background; an onboarding screen must orient first-time users before asking anything of them.
**Apply it:**
- Add a two-slide setup covering who, where, and what the design must do.
- Use comparison or contrast to place the design against something the audience already knows.
- Cut the raw fact list; lead with one anecdote that earns the remaining details.
**Watch out:** Too much exposition breeds apathy; too little leaves readers filling gaps wrongly.
**Pairs with:** 28 Foreshadowing, 94 Tone and Tenor, 52 Mise en Scène

### 28. Foreshadowing
**In one line:** Hinting that something important is coming, so later reveals land as planned payoffs.
**Use it when:** A roadmap deck must preview scalability; a case study builds to ROI; a launch page teases features ahead of the full reveal.
**Apply it:**
- Plant the most critical clue early, ideally in the first slide or paragraph.
- Mark hints as direct (explicit promise) or indirect (scattered detail) in your outline.
- Keep the clue a tease: signal importance without spoiling the actual answer to come.
**Watch out:** Revealing too much turns suspense into a spoiler; hiding everything confuses instead.
**Pairs with:** 27 Exposition, 85 The Paradox of Suspense, 16 Deceptive Cadence

### 37. Hyperbole
**In one line:** Deliberate exaggeration that makes a mundane point vivid without being taken literally.
**Use it when:** A spec sheet's numbers feel flat; ad copy needs a memorable line; a benefit is real but too small to register in absolute terms.
**Apply it:**
- Rewrite one bland benefit as an obvious overstatement, like calling a mattress 'sleeping on a cloud.'
- Anchor the exaggeration to a genuine underlying feeling so the point still reads true.
- Pair that line with real numbers nearby so credibility stays intact.
**Watch out:** If an audience could believe it literally, the claim looks dishonest, not clever.
**Pairs with:** 51 Metaphors, 80 Synecdoche, 75 Stickiness

### 38. In Medias Res
**In one line:** Starting the story in the middle of the action, then working backward to context.
**Use it when:** A pitch opens with the prototype instead of the team intro; a case study starts with the saved patient; attention is fading fast.
**Apply it:**
- Cut introductory slides; open on a working prototype, video testimonial, or outcome.
- Hint that significant events happened before this moment, then flash back to them.
- Hold one narrator's voice throughout so the jump stays coherent.
**Watch out:** Skipping the setup without plotting it first leaves critical gaps the audience can't fill.
**Pairs with:** 28 Foreshadowing, 85 The Paradox of Suspense, 57 Narrative Transportation

### 43. Kill Your Darlings
**In one line:** Removing favored content and features that don't serve the story or the design.
**Use it when:** A deck has redundant slides; a homepage carries too many features; you're attached to a clever phrase that stalls the point.
**Apply it:**
- List every element, then rank: must-have, competitive differentiator, wow; cut the rest.
- Delete repetition: if a point is made once and not exaggerated, remove the echo.
- Hand the draft to someone unattached to cut what you can't.
**Watch out:** Sentimentality keeps clutter alive; if cutting hurts, it's probably subjective, so get a second opinion.
**Pairs with:** 32 Gestalt, 29 Form and Function, 42 Kano and Storytelling

### 45. Lexicon
**In one line:** A curated list of words that describe your design and sound right to its audience.
**Use it when:** Teams use inconsistent terms for the same thing; marketing and engineering talk past each other; a new product needs naming rules.
**Apply it:**
- Build a glossary of approved terms, noting the audience demographics and the jargon to avoid.
- Fix known confusions, like cement versus concrete or beams versus columns, with usage examples.
- Drop the lexicon into every artifact: decks, scrum notes, specs, and marketing copy.
**Watch out:** Organizational jargon alienates the very audience the lexicon should include.
**Pairs with:** 51 Metaphors, 35 Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions Theory, 23 Empathy

### 51. Metaphors
**In one line:** A figure of speech that states one thing is another to make an abstract idea concrete.
**Use it when:** A value proposition stays abstract; brainstorming is stuck on one framing; a visual identity needs a clear comparison to communicate.
**Apply it:**
- Write the concept as a familiar comparison, like 'less is more,' then test comprehension.
- Try the three visual forms: juxtapose, fuse, or replace the design with its comparison.
- Keep the metaphor a supporting vehicle that clarifies the premise, never the star of the story.
**Watch out:** An out-of-context metaphor invites contradictory readings, like less money being better.
**Pairs with:** 3 Analogy, 80 Synecdoche, 45 Lexicon

### 52. Mise en Scène
**In one line:** Staging the environment, props, lighting, and people around a design the way a scene is set.
**Use it when:** Presenting a product in its real context; a service journey needs tangible touchpoints; a demo video must feel lived-in rather than staged in a void.
**Apply it:**
- Describe the actual usage setting: name the kitchen, airport, or clinic where the design lives.
- Choose props, lighting, and presenter attire that reinforce the story's meaning.
- Cast the actors: who uses, builds, and supports the design, with persona detail.
**Watch out:** Staging detached from the design's real environment makes the story feel like theater.
**Pairs with:** 73 Show, Don't Tell, 100 World-Building, 59 Performance

### 60. Personas
**In one line:** Fictional characters built from observed real users who stand in for your audience.
**Use it when:** A brief says 'users' with no face; research exists but nobody reads it; stakeholders dispute who the design is meant to serve.
**Apply it:**
- Build primary, secondary, and stakeholder personas only from observed research, never from invention or guesswork.
- Give each persona a photo, age, values, likes, dislikes, and the challenge your design meets.
- Insert the persona slide immediately after the problem statement, before any solution appears.
**Watch out:** Invented personas make clients unable to recognize themselves, destroying credibility instantly.
**Pairs with:** 5 Archetypes, 9 Character Relatability, 23 Empathy

### 63. Plot
**In one line:** The chain of causes and effects that moves a story from setup to resolution.
**Use it when:** A deck reads like a feature summary; a case study has events but no throughline; a pitch loses momentum halfway through.
**Apply it:**
- Rewrite the deck as linked events: each slide's point triggers the next one.
- Confirm the three Cs are present before calling it a plot: conflict, characters, causation.
- Mark where the design itself acts as a character driving change.
**Watch out:** A summary with no causation stays a list; connective tissue is the plot.
**Pairs with:** 11 Conflict, 93 Three-Act Structure, 62 Pixar's Story Structure

### 73. Show, Don't Tell
**In one line:** Lead with vivid sensory detail instead of flat factual claims, so audiences feel the design.
**Use it when:** A product page lists specs; a pitch describes benefits abstractly; a case study reports results without any scene or texture.
**Apply it:**
- Replace each adjective-claim with a scene: what the user sees, hears, and touches.
- Swap 'it has a good grip handle' for 'peeling an apple with a cloud.'
- Rewrite the hero section so the first sentence shows a real outcome already in progress.
**Watch out:** Descriptive lists starve the imagination; telling alone leaves audiences unmoved.
**Pairs with:** 87 The Senses, 52 Mise en Scène, 96 Verisimilitude

### 80. Synecdoche
**In one line:** Using a part to stand for the whole, or the whole for one part.
**Use it when:** A technical system is too large to explain in full; a campaign wants a shorthand symbol; copy needs economical phrasing for tight headline space.
**Apply it:**
- Name one signature component that stands in for the entire product in short headlines.
- Write container-for-contents lines, like asking whether a user pays with plastic.
- Favor the phrasing audiences already pass along; test which descriptor sticks and spreads.
**Watch out:** An obscure part chosen as the symbol leaves the audience guessing at the whole thing.
**Pairs with:** 51 Metaphors, 3 Analogy, 37 Hyperbole

### 88. The Trigger or Wicked Problem
**In one line:** The underlying why behind a project: the messy, high-stakes problem that makes design necessary.
**Use it when:** Opening a proposal by justifying the work; a stakeholder asks why this matters; framing social or brand risk before committing budget.
**Apply it:**
- Open with the trigger: name the fiscal, social, or brand crisis if nothing changes.
- State that the problem has no single correct formulation, only better or worse responses.
- Frame the work as improving the situation rather than delivering a final solution.
**Watch out:** Overclaiming a definitive fix for a wicked problem invites expert pushback immediately.
**Pairs with:** 11 Conflict, 70 Sensemaking, 41 Jobs to Be Done

### 89. The Wall
**In one line:** The invisible barrier between storyteller and audience; breaking it means addressing them directly.
**Use it when:** A skeptical CFO interrupts your flow; attention drifts mid-presentation; you need to invite questions on purpose or hand over to a teammate.
**Apply it:**
- Pause the narrative to address one named stakeholder directly with a targeted reassurance.
- Hand the mic to a teammate to reset rhythm, then return to the story.
- Mark where you left off so the audience rejoins the thread cleanly.
**Watch out:** Clumsily executed breaks read as disorganization and can overshadow the narrative itself.
**Pairs with:** 59 Performance, 64 Presentations, 21 Dramaturgy, 73 Show, Don't Tell

### 94. Tone and Tenor
**In one line:** Tone is the mood of a message; tenor is the constant subject beneath it.
**Use it when:** A brand voice drifts between playful and serious; a report needs gravitas; stakeholders can't articulate what a design feels like or means.
**Apply it:**
- Write one line defining the tenor (the subject that never changes) in the brief.
- Then choose a tone per touchpoint: playful in onboarding, serious in billing.
- Audit copy, color, and imagery for tone shifts that contradict the tenor.
**Watch out:** Letting the tone drift is fine; letting the tenor drift breaks the message's meaning.
**Pairs with:** 45 Lexicon, 68 Rhythm and Silence, 54 Music, 27 Exposition

### 96. Verisimilitude
**In one line:** The appearance of being true: enough realism that an audience accepts the story as credible.
**Use it when:** A persona sounds too perfect to be real; projections promise too much; a bold concept needs plausibility for skeptical reviewers.
**Apply it:**
- Describe details no one can reasonably dispute: the actual setting, tools, and constraints.
- After any bold claim, re-ground the audience with something recognizable and common.
- Keep the narrative consistent with its own premise; generic verisimilitude breaks the moment it swerves.
**Watch out:** A persona nobody has ever met destroys credibility the instant they appear.
**Pairs with:** 60 Personas, 23 Empathy, 73 Show, Don't Tell
