# History and Symbols

Everything in this file is design research for making artwork. It is not a
philatelic catalogue. Dates, values, and colours vary between catalogues, so treat
the tables as a working map, not the final word.

## How Ethiopia's Stamps Began

- **Before stamps.** Ethiopian messages travelled by courier. The *melektegnas*
  carried letters attached to a stick and ran between towns.
- **1867-1868.** The British field post office at Massawa used stamps of British
  India during the Magdala campaign.
- **1875-1876.** Egypt took Harar and opened a post office. Letters used Egyptian
  stamps cancelled with a Maltese cross.
- **9 March 1894.** Emperor Menelik II awarded a concession to Swiss engineer
  Alfred Ilg to build a railway and develop postal services.
- **1894.** Ilg arranged for Leon Chefneux to contract the engraver
  Louis-Eugene Mouchon. Mouchon designed seven stamps: four portraits of
  Menelik II and three heraldic lions. The Atelier de Fabrication des
  Timbres-Postes in Paris printed them, along with four postcard values.
- **29 January 1895.** Earliest known use of the first issue. The stamps were
  valid for local mail and for mail to Djibouti.
- **Values.** The first issue was denominated in guerches: 1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4, 8,
  and 16. Sixteen guerches made one Menelik dollar.
- **1901.** Local overprints appeared to stop dealers from reselling discounted
  stamps.
- **1908.** Ethiopia joined the Universal Postal Union (UPU), followed by an
  overprinted set.
- **1909.** New designs inscribed `POSTES ETHIOPIENNES`, still with guerche values.
- **1917.** Overprints marked the coronation and regency of Empress Zewditu.
- **1919.** A fifteen-stamp set showed Zewditu and Tafari Makonnen (the future
  Haile Selassie I) with Ethiopian animals, inscribed `ETHIOPIE`.
- **1928.** A new Zewditu and Tafari set, plus overprints for the opening of the
  General Post Office and for Tafari's coronation as Negus.
- **1930.** Overprints and seven coronation stamps marked Haile Selassie I's
  proclamation and coronation, with the coronation monument and imperial symbols.
- **1936-1941.** Italian occupation. Italy issued `ETIOPIA` stamps with King
  Victor Emmanuel III, then stamps of Italian East Africa. Treat this material as
  colonial propaganda, not as a design to celebrate.
- **1941.** Liberation. A new Haile Selassie series followed in 1942, and a
  provincial capitals series in 1943.
- **1946.** The first pictorial commemorative set showed Ethiopian scenes.
- **1949.** The UPU 75th anniversary set (four values) is a useful model for the
  mid-century look: oval portrait, globe and cherub, `PAR AVION`, bilingual text,
  and the dates 1874 and 1949.
- **1950s-1960s.** Photogravure and lithography brought full colour: wildlife,
  Olympic Games, education, health, aviation, and state anniversaries.
- **1974.** The Derg removed the monarchy. Stamp art turned to red, yellow, and
  green fields, workers and soldiers, land reform, and revolution anniversaries.
- **1991 onward.** The Federal Democratic Republic issued tourism, coffee,
  UNESCO, sports, and leaders commemoratives. The 1994 centenary celebrated the
  first stamps.
- **Recent issues.** Modern stamps are typically offset lithography. A 2024
  commemorative for the runner Derartu Tulu was issued at 50 birr.

## Denomination Timeline

| Period | Currency | Values seen | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1894-1930s | Guerche (G) | 1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 | 16 guerches = 1 Menelik dollar. Early stamps put the value in a circle at the bottom |
| 1940s-1960s | Ethiopian dollar and cents (c, $) | 1c to 50c, $1 | The 1949 UPU set uses 5c, 15c, 25c, 50c |
| 1970s-now | Birr (ብር) and santim (ሳንቲም) | 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 50, 60 santim; 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 birr | One birr equals 100 santim. Modern commemoratives often carry 50 birr |

**Rendering the value.** Historical values use small serif numerals with the unit
abbreviation (`5c`, `50c`, `1 G`). Modern values use a clean numeral plus the
Amharic currency word (`5 ብር`, `50 ሳንቲም`). Keep the value inside the frame, never
floating on the paper margin.

## What the Real Stamps Teach

- **1894 Menelik portrait.** Dense engraved foliage fills the border. The head is
  in profile or three-quarter view. Ge'ez text wraps the top and sides. The value
  sits in a small circle at the bottom. The engraver's signature sits in tiny
  letters at the edge.
- **1894 Lion of Judah.** The lion fills a circular medallion, flag and cross in
  paw, with the motto arc above. Fine cross-hatching gives the mane and muscles.
- **1896 postage due.** The same frames, overprinted with a large `T` and new
  values. This is the origin of the overprint tradition.
- **1949 UPU.** Landscape orientation. A globe and cherub on the left, a portrait
  oval on the right, `PAR AVION` at the top, the country name and Amharic text in
  bands at the bottom, dates 1874 and 1949 in the corners. Four values share one
  frame and change colour.
- **1960s commemoratives.** A portrait medallion and a local scene sit side by
  side inside an ornate frame. Colour is richer but still lithographic.
- **Derg era.** A red field, two soldiers, a yellow Amharic slogan, a green and
  yellow `ETHIOPIA` band, and the value in a corner. The design is a poster on a
  stamp.
- **Modern leaders series.** Flat vector portraits, a gold frame, a faint map,
  a bilingual header, and the value in both Amharic and Latin. This is the
  clearest model for a contemporary look.

## Cultural and Ethical Notes

- Treat the Lion of Judah, the cross, the crown, and imperial and religious
  figures with dignity. Use humour on everyday subjects instead.
- Do not make political propaganda or celebrate the Italian occupation. Study
  colonial stamps critically and label them as historical material.
- Do not present a concept stamp as a real issue. Put "concept stamp" or "not a
  real postal issue" in the caption.
- Avoid poverty stereotypes, conflict imagery, and generic "African" motifs.
  Name the place, the species, the food, and the craft.
- Verify Amharic before publishing. Image models often produce attractive but
  wrong Ge'ez.
- For living people, get permission before using a likeness. Public-domain
  historical portraits are safer.
- Ethiopian names and spellings vary in transliteration. Pick one form and keep
  it consistent: Menelik II, Zewditu, Haile Selassie I, Tafari Makonnen.
